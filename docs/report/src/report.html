1.1 Problem statement

Pedestrians account for about 23 % of road deaths worldwide [1], and in Kazakhstan collisions at crossings, loading areas and along the kerb remain frequent because drivers notice pedestrians too late [2]. Large vehicles make the problem worse: the high cabin of a truck or bus creates blind spots at the nearside and directly in front of the vehicle, so a person standing in a loading bay, next to a parked lorry or on the kerb beside a turning truck is often invisible to the driver until the last moment. In the survey conducted for this project (Section 2) 61 % of respondents named high truck/bus cabins as one of the main reasons why drivers fail to notice pedestrians, and 92 % had experienced a near-miss at a crossing.

Existing counter-measures are either passive (lighting, LED markings, signage — they improve visibility but do not analyse the scene) or expensive and slow to deploy (fixed CCTV with analytics servers, estimated at $15 000–50 000 per site in Kazakhstan [3]; retrofit on-vehicle sensor kits that protect one vehicle only). There is no cheap, quickly deployable way to monitor a specific dangerous area — a loading bay, a yard gate, a construction-site exit, the kerb at a crossing — and to raise an alert the moment a person steps into it.

1.2 Target users

  • Safety staff of logistics yards, warehouses, construction sites and bus depots who need to know when a person enters an area where trucks manoeuvre and cannot see them.
  • Municipal / road-safety researchers who want to measure how often pedestrians enter a risk zone at a given crossing before deciding on infrastructure changes.
  • Developers and students who need a reproducible, open pipeline (same code live and offline) for evaluating zone-membership rules on public datasets.

1.3 Aim

To develop and evaluate a low-cost real-time system that turns ordinary smartphones into camera nodes, detects pedestrians with an object-detection network, decides whether each person is inside a configured danger zone using a foot-point geometric rule, and delivers entry/exit events to a web dashboard (and, in future, to a driver-facing warning device) with sub-second latency.

1.4 Objectives

  1. Analyse existing pedestrian-safety and video-analytics solutions and formulate requirements grounded in user evidence (survey, discussions with drivers).
  2. Design a three-tier architecture: phone browser (capture) → laptop backend (FastAPI, YOLOv8n, geometric rules) → web dashboard; record every key decision as an ADR.
  3. Implement secure phone pairing (PIN/QR, opaque device tokens, HTTPS/WSS), a per-camera latest-frame scheduler, person detection, six zone-membership rules computed every frame, a hysteresis state machine producing zone_entered/zone_cleared events, and a dashboard with a polygon zone editor, live overlays, events and CSV export.
  4. Build an evaluation harness that runs the same detector and rules offline on public datasets (MOT17, WILDTRACK) and on own recordings, with pre-registered metrics and success criteria.
  5. Measure precision/recall/F1 of foot-point rules against the bounding-box-overlap baseline, and tail latency of the latest-frame scheduler against a FIFO queue with several phones.
  6. Document, test (unit, integration, security, CV, end-to-end, load) and demonstrate the system; prepare the approbation report.

1.5 Expected outcomes

  • A working local system (one command to set up, one to run) with 218+ automated tests, documented in the repository.
  • Quantitative answer to the research question: whether foot-anchored zone membership reduces false alarms compared with bounding-box overlap, and at what cost in recall (target: precision ≥ baseline + 0.10 with recall loss ≤ 0.05).
  • Measured latency profile: server-side p50 ≤ 150 ms and p99 ≤ 400 ms with three phones at 10 fps on a laptop; fairness ratio ≤ 1.3 between cameras.
  • Reusable architecture decisions (9 ADRs), diagrams and an evaluation protocol that other teams can repeat.

2. Evidence of relevance

Two sources of user evidence inform the requirements: a structured online survey of pedestrians and drivers (n = 36) and informal discussions with drivers. Statistical context comes from WHO road-safety reports [1, 2]. Findings that are directly measured are separated from assumptions in Section 2.4.

2.1 Survey of pedestrians and drivers (n = 36)

A Google Forms questionnaire with eight closed questions and one open question was distributed digitally among residents of Astana (students, colleagues, acquaintances; convenience sample). It covered (1) pedestrian experience at unsignalised crossings, (2) driver experience and (3) attitude towards automatic warning systems. 36 people answered the pedestrian section; the driver-specific questions were answered by 10 respondents. Raw results are available in the form’s response summary (screenshots in Appendix A).

Key findings. 86 % of respondents cross unsignalised crossings daily or several times a week; 72 % feel anxious or stressed there; 91.7 % have experienced a driver noticing them late or failing to yield. The most cited visibility obstacles are parked cars blocking the view (69.4 %), high truck/bus cabins — blind spots (61.1 %) and driver distraction (61.1 %). 88.6 % strongly support automatic sensors that trigger a warning when a person appears; nobody opposed. Open answers repeat three themes: visible driver alerts, better lighting, and enforcement. One driver wrote: “safety at crossings can be improved by using smart camera systems that detect pedestrians in real time and automatically alert drivers” — which is exactly the function public.safe implements.

3. Literature review

The review covers four groups of sources: (1) road-safety statistics and policy, (2) passive crossing infrastructure (lighting, LED markings, warning beacons), (3) real-time person detection on edge hardware, (4) geometric and tracking methods used to decide where a detected person is. The full list has 15 sources, of which 11 are peer-reviewed publications; the core detection and edge-computing sources are from 2021–2026, while foundational works (point-in-polygon algorithm, crowd analysis survey) are retained.

3.1 Passive infrastructure improves visibility but does not analyse the scene

Before-after studies of crosswalk warning systems (“SeeMe”, Norway) show that pedestrian-activated flashing beacons increase driver yielding [3]; LED-lit crosswalks and integrated lighting-warning systems improve night-time yielding at unsignalised crossings [4, 5]. All three studies measure driver behaviour, not detection: the systems either react to a push button / simple presence sensor or light the crossing permanently. They do not know whether a person is actually in the dangerous area, cannot distinguish a person from a parked bicycle, and cannot be moved to a loading bay or yard. This is the gap public.safe addresses with class-aware detection and a configurable polygon.

3.2 Real-time person detection at the edge is feasible on commodity hardware

YOLO-family detectors dominate real-time surveillance work. Bharadwaj et al. apply YOLO to edge surveillance for human detection [6]; Maltezos et al. report 70 ms per frame for YOLOv5 person detection on embedded GPUs in a distributed edge platform with IoT alert sharing, confirming the latency advantage of edge inference over centralised pipelines [11]. FPGA deployments [12] and EdgeML traffic management systems [14] reach sub-200 ms latency on commodity hardware. Night-time variants (YOLOv8-night) show that low-light performance is a known weakness that can be mitigated by training data and architecture changes [13]. The US DOT review of cost-effective computer-vision solutions for transportation [15] explicitly recommends consumer cameras as a cheap alternative to stationary sensor networks. Ultralytics YOLOv8 [8] is the detector used here (nano variant: 37.3 mAP on COCO at 3.2 M parameters).

3.3 Deciding where a person is: bounding-box overlap versus ground-contact points

Most zone-intrusion analytics decide membership by intersecting the detection box with the zone (IoU or any-overlap) [6, 7]. The failure mode is systematic: a person standing next to the zone has the upper part of the box over it while the feet are outside, which produces a false alarm. Crowd-analysis work uses ground-plane projection of the box bottom instead [9]; the classical ray-casting point-in-polygon algorithm [10] gives an O(n) exact test for that point. Multi-object trackers such as ByteTrack [7] add temporal identity but still rely on the box geometry for position. The research work that precedes this project found that a three-point foot-contact rule raised precision from 0.71 to 0.94 in laboratory scenarios; however, the published ablation is not consistent with an “any of three points” rule (a superset rule cannot have fewer positives than its subset), which is why experiment E2 re-establishes the result with all six rules computed on the same frames (ADR 0008).

3.4 What the project takes from the literature

  • Use a nano-scale YOLO detector on the laptop GPU/CPU; do not attempt on-phone inference (browser WebGPU inference was excluded as too slow and battery-hungry for continuous use).
  • Treat bounding-box overlap as the baseline and foot-anchored rules as the proposed method; report precision/recall with confidence intervals and a paired test (McNemar), as in [11, 14].
  • Bound tail latency explicitly (per-camera latest-frame mailbox) rather than relying on average throughput.
  • Keep identity out of the pipeline (no faces, no tracking IDs persisted) — privacy requirements in [15] and RK law.

4.1 Conclusions from the comparison

  • On-vehicle kits protect only the vehicle they are installed in and cost a retrofit per vehicle; they cannot cover a yard where many third-party trucks operate. A site-centred system that watches the dangerous area itself is complementary and much cheaper.
  • Fixed-camera analytics decide membership by box intersection (Hikvision, Axis) or by the box bottom-centre (Frigate). None of them lets the operator choose and compare rules, and none publishes precision/recall — which is why the project treats the rule itself as the research object and measures it.
  • Only Frigate is open and reproducible, but it needs installed IP cameras and a server; it has no pairing flow for ad-hoc phone cameras and no offline evaluation on public datasets.
  • Crosswalk warning systems prove that a visible signal changes driver behaviour [3, 5]; public.safe produces the event that such a signal needs, from a camera instead of a push button.

7.3 Component diagram (draft)

Processing path. The phone encodes a JPEG and sends it over the camera WebSocket. The ingest handler puts it into that camera’s LatestFrameSlot without decoding; an unread older frame is replaced and counted. One inference worker thread takes frames round-robin across cameras, decodes, runs YOLOv8n (person class only), computes all six membership rules for every detection and every enabled zone, feeds the active rule’s count into the zone state machine, persists events and metric samples, and publishes a frame_result JSON (plus the JPEG unless privacy mode) to subscribed dashboards. The dashboard draws overlays on a canvas from that JSON. Every stage is timed (timings in the frame result) so latency can be attributed.

9.2 Outcome metrics

  • Per-person zone membership (each matched detection × rule): TP/FP/FN/TN → precision, recall, F1, FPR; detector misses reported separately as fn_missed_detection so rule errors are distinguishable from detector errors.
  • Event level: zone_entered events matched within ±10 frames → event precision/recall/F1, timing error median and p90, duplicate-event rate.
  • Statistics: 5 000-sample bootstrap 95 % CIs (seed 1234); exact McNemar test on paired per-person outcomes for each pair of rules; boundary analysis — error rate per 10 px bin of the ground-truth foot distance to the polygon edge.
  • Latency: server-side spans (queue wait, decode, inference, rules, fan-out) as p50/p90/p95/p99; end-to-end estimate from phone timestamps with RTT-based uncertainty; fairness ratio = max ÷ min processed fps across cameras; drop ratio.

9.3 Baselines, datasets and measurement conditions

Baseline rulebbox_overlap — any intersection of the person box with the zone, the rule used by commercial analytics [6, 7]. Secondary baseline: bottom_center (one foot point, as in Frigate).
Baseline schedulerShared unbounded FIFO queue (SCHEDULER_STRATEGY=fifo), the design of the research prototype.
DatasetsMOT17 train sequences 02, 04, 09 (1920×1080, 25–30 fps, full-body ground-truth boxes; 04 is a night street from an elevated viewpoint) — primary; WILDTRACK (7 calibrated cameras, ground-plane positions) — geometric check with a metric zone; own recordings via EVAL_RECORD=1 for the four lab scenarios plus night, occlusion and tangential approach, with consent of people filmed.
Zone ground truthThree polygons per sequence (strip, corner, irregular) committed as JSON; per-person in_zone labels produced with publicsafe eval annotate every 5th frame by two annotators; Cohen’s κ reported; auto-derived labels used only for sanity checks.
ConditionsDetector YOLOv8n, conf 0.30, imgsz 640, FP32; weights SHA-256 recorded; seeds fixed; run.json with host, device, package versions, git commit and config hash in every output directory. Live tests: Apple Silicon laptop, 5 GHz Wi-Fi, phones at 10 fps, 1280×720, JPEG quality 65.
